
## Course Overview

Introduction to the Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labeling of Chemicals (GHS) for semiconductor employees.

### Learning Objectives

After completing this course, you will be able to:

- Identify what is included in the GHS
- Recognize the characteristics of hazardous chemicals
- Recall GHS classifications

---

## 1. Semiconductor Chemical Safety Introduction

The semiconductor fabrication process uses many hazardous materials in manufacturing integrated circuits and devices. These hazardous materials are stored and used as liquid chemicals, liquefied and pressurized gases, and solid substances.

Due to the safety controls in place, the semiconductor industry has a very good chemical safety record.

### Employee Responsibilities

- Know, understand, and follow safety procedures
- Report injuries, accidents, and unsafe conditions immediately to EHS or your supervisor
- Never take risks or shortcuts
- If something feels unsafe, stop and think before taking action
- Properly use all machinery, tools, substances, and PPE
- Always immediately evacuate during an emergency

### Employer Responsibilities

- Develop a chemical safety management program (processes, equipment, hazard control measures)
- Identify all hazardous chemicals in the workplace (hazardous chemical inventory)
- Communicate chemical hazards to employees via SDSs, signs, and labels

---

## 2. Globally Harmonized System (GHS)

### Purpose

Define and classify the hazards of chemical products and communicate health and safety information on labels and Safety Data Sheets (SDSs).

### What GHS Does

- Standardizes an international approach to hazard communication
- OSHA's Hazard Communication Standard (HazCom) in the US meets GHS requirements
- Harmonizes definitions of hazardous chemicals
- Sets specific criteria for labels
- Sets a specific 16-section format for SDSs
- Helps employees better understand hazards

---

## 3. Physical States of Chemicals

### GHS Definitions

| State | Definition |
|-------|-----------|
| **Gas** | Vapor pressure > 300 kPa at 50°C, or completely gaseous at 20°C at standard pressure (101.3 kPa) |
| **Liquid** | Vapor pressure ≤ 300 kPa at 50°C, not completely gaseous at 20°C, melting point ≤ 20°C at standard pressure |
| **Solid** | Does not meet the definitions of gas or liquid |

## 4. Three Types of Hazards

1. **Physical hazards** — corrosivity, flammability, explosive potential
2. **Health hazards** — adverse effects on health (short-term or long-term)
3. **Environmental hazards** — pollution of rivers, streams, lakes; endangering aquatic life

### GHS Pictograms

Nine standardized pictograms: black hazard symbol on white background within a red diamond frame. Each conveys specific information about a chemical's hazards.

## 5. Physical Hazards

### 5.1 Flammable Liquids

Give off flammable vapors that ignite at temperatures not more than 93°C (190°F) — this is the **flash point**.

Examples in semiconductor industry:
- Isopropyl alcohol
- Acetone
- Photoresists
- Mineral-based pump oil

### 5.2 Flammable Solids

Readily combustible or may cause/contribute to fire through friction. Ignite by flame, friction, or reaction with water.

- **Example:** Phosphorus (byproduct found in ion implanters)

### 5.3 Flammable Gases

Must mix with oxygen to ignite. Flammable range determined by LEL (Lower Explosive Limit) and UEL (Upper Explosive Limit).

Common flammable gases:
- Hydrogen
- Methane
- Acetylene
- Arsine

### 5.4 Pyrophoric Substances

Ignite simply by coming into contact with air. No flame or other ignition source needed.

Examples:
- Phosphine
- Silane
- Organometallic substances (e.g., dimethyl aluminum hydride)

**Important:** Cannot be extinguished using water or CO₂. Some extinguishing materials can cause explosions due to incompatibility.

### 5.5 Self-Heating / Self-Reactive Chemicals

- **Self-heating:** Chemicals that heat up on their own when exposed to air. Can reach ignition temperature if stored improperly.
- **Self-reactive:** Chemicals that can decompose violently or explode when heated.

### 5.6 Chemicals That Emit Flammable Gas

Some chemicals produce flammable gas when they contact water. The gas may ignite spontaneously or be ignited by a spark.

### 5.7 Explosives

Can detonate or explode under certain conditions. Extremely sensitive to heat, shock, or friction.

### 5.8 Gases Under Pressure

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Compressed gas | Entirely gaseous at -50°C, packaged under pressure |
| Liquefied gas | Partially liquid at -50°C, packaged under pressure |
| Refrigerated liquefied gas | Partially liquid because of its low temperature |
| Dissolved gas | Dissolved in a liquid phase solvent |

**Hazard:** If a gas cylinder is damaged, it has the potential to become an unguided missile.

### 5.9 Oxidizers

- Do not burn, but make fuel burn better
- Can cause or contribute to combustion of other material
- Some are sensitive to heat and may rapidly react or explode
- Some (e.g., nitric acid) are powerful corrosives
- Others (e.g., hydrogen peroxide, chlorine) can burn skin and mucous membranes
- Concentrated nitric acid may cause fires on contact with flammable solvents (wood, paper)

## 6. Health Hazards

### 6.1 Toxic Chemicals

May produce acute (short-term) or chronic (long-term) health effects. Can cause death. Includes chemicals that can cause cancer or acute toxicity.

### 6.2 Corrosive Chemicals

| Effect | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| Corrosive to skin | Irreversible skin damage |
| Eye damage | Serious eye irritation and damage |
| Respiratory damage | Irritates nose/throat, can cause respiratory tract damage |
| Corrosive to metal | Can materially damage or destroy metals |

#### pH Scale

| pH | Classification | Examples |
|----|---------------|----------|
| ≤ 2 | Corrosive acid | Sulfuric, hydrochloric, nitric acid |
| 7 | Neutral | Water |
| ≥ 11.5 | Corrosive base (alkali) | Ammonium hydroxide, TMAH |

### 6.3 Hydrofluoric Acid (HF) — Special Hazard

Commonly used for etching and chamber cleaning. Excellent at dissolving silicon compounds but extremely dangerous when used improperly.

**Acute effects:**
- Extreme respiratory irritation
- Immediate and severe eye damage
- Pulmonary edema
- Concentrated (>50%): immediate severe penetrating burns
- Dilute solutions: symptoms can be **delayed up to 24 hours**

**First aid:** Rinse with water for 5 minutes → Apply calcium gluconate → Seek immediate medical attention.

### 6.4 Carcinogens

Chemicals suspected of causing cancer or increasing cancer incidence.

- Examples: Arsenic, gallium arsenide, chloroform

### 6.5 Mutagens

Substances that can alter or mutate DNA.

- Example: Ionizing radiation (in semiconductor fabrication)
- Some chemical mixtures for plasma etching may be carcinogens and mutagens

### 6.6 Reproductive Toxins

Adverse effects on sexual function, fertility, and developmental toxicity in offspring.

- Examples: Gallium arsenide, nitrous oxide

### 6.7 Respiratory Sensitizers

Produce allergy-like reactions upon inhalation. Reactions worsen with repeated exposure.

- Example: Silicon aluminum oxynitride

### 6.8 Aspiration Toxins

Cause acute health effects (chemical pneumonia, lung injury) from entry of liquids/solids through oral or nasal cavity.

- Examples: Aluminum oxide, aqua regia, hydrogen sulfide

### 6.9 Target Organ Toxins

Cause significant damage to bodily organs after single or repeated exposure.

| Chemical | Target Organ |
|----------|-------------|
| Copper chloride | Liver |
| Nitrogen trifluoride | Kidney |
| Methyl fluoride | Narcotic effects, respiratory tract irritation |

### 6.10 Skin/Eye Irritants

Produce reversible damage upon contact.

- Diluted acids and bases
- Solvents (isopropyl alcohol, acetone)
- Isocyanates (used for packaging)

### 6.11 Skin Sensitizers

Induce allergic response following skin contact. Reactions worsen with successive contact.

- Example: Potassium hydroxide (found in adhesives)

---

## 7. Environmental Hazards

Chemicals that may cause damage to the aquatic environment (short-term or long-term).

- Examples: Halon (in fire extinguishers), silver, zinc oxide
- Environmental hazard pictogram is required in the EU but not in the US
