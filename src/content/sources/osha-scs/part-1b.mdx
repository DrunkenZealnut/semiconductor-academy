
## Course Overview

This course covers communication, controls, and emergency procedures for hazardous chemicals in the semiconductor industry. Topics include Safety Data Sheets (SDSs), labels, hazardous chemical controls, emergency situations, and hazardous chemical disposal.

### Learning Objectives

After completing this course, you will be able to:

- Identify SDS and label requirements according to the Globally Harmonized System (GHS)
- Recognize what controls to use and precautions to take when storing and using hazardous chemicals
- Recall what to do when there is an emergency situation involving hazardous chemicals
- Identify disposal requirements and restrictions for hazardous chemicals

---

## 1. Hazardous Materials

Hazardous materials include liquid chemicals, liquefied and pressurized gases, and solid substances. Throughout this program, they are referred to as "chemicals," with exceptions when discussing safe use and handling specifics for liquid chemicals, gases, and solids.

Key questions addressed:

- How do you know if a chemical you work with has hazardous characteristics?
- Where can you find information about how to protect yourself?
- What should you do if you've been exposed to a hazardous chemical?

---

## 2. Safety Data Sheets (SDS)

### 2.1 SDS Structure

There are 16 sections in a chemical Safety Data Sheet, falling into four broad categories:

| Category | Sections |
|----------|----------|
| **Identification** | Identification of substance/mixture, supplier, hazards, composition, ingredients |
| **Emergency & Exposure** | Routes of exposure, first aid, firefighting, accidental release, handling, storage, exposure controls |
| **Properties** | Physical/chemical properties, stability, reactivity, toxicological information |
| **Additional Information** | Ecological impact, disposal, transport, regulation, SDS preparation/revision |

### 2.2 SDS Requirements (GHS)

- The chemical manufacturer, importer, or distributor must provide SDSs with the first shipment and anytime the information changes.
- If you don't receive an SDS, your employer must contact the supplier, manufacturer, or importer to obtain one and maintain a record of the contact.
- Your employer must obtain SDSs for all hazardous chemicals in the workplace before you use them.
- SDSs have to be easy to access. It is your responsibility to know exactly where they are.
- SDSs must be in English or in the official language of the country (additional languages may be provided).
- SDSs must include the specific identity of the chemical, common names, and contact information of the responsible organization.

## 3. Labels

### 3.1 Label Requirements

- Labels need to be legible so that people can read them.
- They need to be in English or in the official language of the country (may be in other languages as well).
- Labels should be prominently displayed so that anyone can easily identify the contents.

### 3.2 GHS Classification System

The GHS classification system rates hazards from 1 to 5:
- **1 = highest hazard**, 5 = lowest hazard
- This is the **opposite** of the NFPA Hazard Rating System (0 = no hazard, 4 = high hazard)

### 3.3 Alternative Labeling Systems

- Employers may label workplace containers with the shipping label or alternatives meeting the standard.
- **NFPA** and **HMIS** systems are still permitted for workplace containers.

NFPA label color codes:
| Color | Hazard |
|-------|--------|
| Blue | Health |
| Red | Flammability |
| Yellow | Instability / Reactivity |
| White | Special hazards (W with strikethrough = water reactive, OX = oxidizing agent) |

## 4. Hierarchy of Hazard Control

Five general methods for controlling hazards, from most to least effective:

### 4.1 Elimination (Most Effective)

Redesigning the process so that the hazard doesn't exist.
- **Example:** Using lead-free solder to eliminate lead exposure.

### 4.2 Substitution

Using a safer process or product instead.
- **Example:** Glycol ethers replaced by less hazardous photolithography chemicals.

### 4.3 Engineering Controls

Designs or modifications to equipment, ventilation systems, and processes that reduce the source of the hazard.

Examples in semiconductor fabrication:
- Mechanical ventilation and exhaust hoods and enclosures
- Automated chemical wet benches (robots handle wafer boats instead of employees)
- Wafer handling systems (automated tracks transfer wafer pods from tool to tool)
- Interlock systems (shut down a tool when doors are open)
- Toxic gas monitoring equipment (shuts down equipment and activates evacuation horns/strobes after detecting a toxic gas leak)

### 4.4 Administrative Controls

Altering the way people work to reduce exposure:
- Employee training
- Control of hazardous energies
- Preventive maintenance on equipment
- Job rotation to lessen exposure time
- Posting signs and warning labels
- Scheduling maintenance, gas bottle changes, or other high-exposure operations for times when few workers are present
- Emergency planning and preparedness
- Chemical handling and storage practices

### 4.5 Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) (Last Resort)

PPE is the **last line of protection**. Only rely on it when other control methods are inadequate or use it with other methods as an added layer.

Examples:
- Supplied air respirator when changing toxic gas cylinders
- Chemical gloves over cleanroom gloves, with apron, face shield and goggles when pouring chemicals into a wet bench
- Steel-toed shoes when handling heavy equipment

## 5. Safe Chemical Handling Practices

- Do not bring food, drinks, chewing gum, or cigarettes into clean rooms.
- Always wash your hands thoroughly with warm water and soap after handling hazardous chemicals.
- Never touch your face or exposed skin before washing your hands.
- Only handle chemicals after proper training and while wearing appropriate PPE.
- Transport bottles in rubber carriers; when carrying more than one bottle, use a chemical cart.
- Limit chemicals at your workstation to the amount needed for the process or a one-day supply.
- Store excess flammable, combustible, and reactive chemicals in approved storage cabinets (designed for chemical separation and spill protection).
- Chemicals have expiration dates. Use by that date or dispose of them properly as hazardous waste.
- Only store flammable chemicals requiring refrigeration in specifically approved explosion-proof refrigerators.

---

## 6. Chemical Storage

Key points for storing chemicals:

- Store incompatible chemicals in separate cabinets or areas.
- Store heavy chemical containers on lower shelves.
- Do not store chemical containers above eye level.
- Chemicals (including those in drums) must be sealed with lids or bungs at all times, except materials that require self-venting caps to release pressure buildup.
- Chemical storage shelves should have raised edges or lips.
- In earthquake-prone areas, special shelving systems may be needed and cabinets should be anchored.
- Avoid storing large quantities of chemicals in the work area. Keep only the amount immediately required.
- Never store chemical containers on work surfaces or in bay passageways.

## 7. Emergency Procedures

### 7.1 Chemical Accident Response

1. Protect your own safety.
2. Do not approach a spill or release unless you know the material hazards and precautions and are trained in spill procedures.
3. Keep others away from the affected area.
4. Call for help if needed.

### 7.2 Chemical Exposure First Aid

| Exposure Type | Action |
|---------------|--------|
| Chemical in face or eyes | Take patient to eye wash station, rinse for **15 minutes** |
| Corrosive/toxic chemical on body, chemical burns, or skin absorption | Take patient to safety shower, rinse for **15 minutes** |
| Inhalation of toxic/corrosive gases or vapors | Safely remove patient from affected location, get to fresh air |

### 7.3 Gas Releases

- Personnel should **immediately evacuate** in the event of a toxic gas alarm.
- Only trained emergency response personnel may respond to gas alarms.
- Inert gases create pressure hazards and can displace oxygen from enclosed spaces. Do not approach inert gas releases; call for emergency response.

### 7.4 Spill Procedures

**Small spills** (within allowable cleanup limits): Semiconductor personnel can clean up if:
- The chemical and hazards are known
- The area is secure and safe (e.g., no ignition sources near flammable vapors)
- Personnel are trained to perform cleanup
- Appropriate cleanup materials and proper disposal receptacles are available
- If any condition is not met, call the local emergency number.

**Large spills** (generally over 1 pint / 470 mL of hazardous chemical):
1. Get away from the spill for your own safety.
2. Get others away from the spill.
3. Prevent personnel from approaching.
4. Contact the local emergency number to report the spill and request response.

## 8. Hazardous Waste Disposal

Hazardous waste poses a threat to employees and the environment if improperly managed. Semiconductor companies produce a variety of hazardous waste subject to strict regulation.

### Types of Semiconductor Hazardous Chemical Waste

- Photolithography waste (acetone, spent photoresists)
- Arsenic-contaminated gloves, wipes, and debris
- Spill cleanup materials
- Waste oils

### Requirements

- The company generating the waste must manage it **from cradle to grave** (from the moment it is generated to ultimate disposal).
- Waste must be disposed of in appropriately labeled hazardous waste containers.
- If unsure whether waste is hazardous or non-hazardous, contact the **Environmental Health and Safety (EHS) Department** or your supervisor.
- Disposal must be handled by **licensed hazardous waste facilities**.
